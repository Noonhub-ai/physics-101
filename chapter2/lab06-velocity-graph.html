Velocity–Time Graph: Area Explorer

Discover why the signed area under a velocity–time graph gives displacement, while the sum of absolute areas gives distance traveled. Based on Example 8, page 21 of your lecture.

1 · Predict before exploring

A car moves forward, then backward, then forward again. Can its total distance be greater than its final displacement? Use the graph to find out.

Core idea
Δx = ∫ v dt   ·   Distance = ∫ |v| dt

For simple shapes, calculate areas of triangles and rectangles. Above the time axis is positive; below is negative. A negative area contributes positively to distance.

Show the full worked solution from the lecture

Region A₁ (0–4 s): triangle above the axis.

A₁ = ½ × 4 × 10 = +20 m

Region A₂ (4–6 s): rectangle below the axis.

A₂ = 2 × (−5) = −10 m

Region A₃ (6–8 s): triangle above the axis.

A₃ = ½ × 2 × 10 = +10 m

Δx = 20 − 10 + 10 = +20 m

Distance = |20| + |−10| + |10| = 40 m

Average velocity = 20 / 8 = +2.5 m/s

Average speed = 40 / 8 = 5 m/s

The vertical line jumps at 4 s and 6 s are not areas; intervals of zero time add no displacement. The graph does not define a unique velocity at those precise jump instants.

Show a worked example

Choose velocities +6, −4, and −2 m/s over three 2-second intervals:

Δx = (6)(2) + (−4)(2) + (−2)(2) = 12 − 8 − 4 = 0 m

Distance = 12 + 8 + 4 = 24 m

The car returns to its starting position, even though it has traveled 24 m.

5 · Quick understanding check

Question: A particle moves at −3 m/s for 4 seconds. What are its displacement and distance?

Think about the sign of velocity and the absolute value of area.
Show step-by-step solution

The signed area is a rectangle of width 4 s and height −3 m/s.

Δx = (−3)(4) = −12 m

Distance = |−12| = 12 m
